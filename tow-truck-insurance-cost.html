How Much Does Tow Truck Insurance Cost?

By Ryan Leavitt · Updated October 2026

No averages pulled from thin air. Here’s what published 2026 rate guides actually say, what moves your number, and how to tell a real quote from a guess.

On this page: The ranges · Why towing costs more · What drives your number · Coverage stack · Why quotes vary · How you pay · Lower the cost

The short answer

Published 2026 rate guides put tow truck insurance at roughly $450 to $2,000 or more per truck per month — about $5,400 to $25,000+ per truck per year. A light-duty truck doing local roadside work sits at the bottom of that range. Heavy recovery, repo, and impound work sits at the top and often above it.

Those are planning figures from published guides, not quotes. Nobody can price your operation from a blog post. Your premium comes from your trucks, your drivers, your radius, and your loss history. Anyone who gives you a firm number without asking about those things is guessing.

Mixed towing with commercial accounts$800–$1,400$9,600–$16,800
Repo, impound, and vehicle storage$1,000–$1,800$12,000–$21,600

Another 2026 guide puts the overall range at $450–$2,000+ per month per truck. The two guides don’t match exactly because they measure different mixes of operations — which is exactly why single “average” numbers online are unreliable. One source’s “average” of $450–$620 a month sits far below the ranges above, most likely because it reflects a narrower or lighter-duty sample. Treat every published figure as a planning range, not a price.

Why towing costs more than a delivery truck

A delivery truck has one exposure: driving. A tow truck has four, and each one gets its own coverage and its own price:

  • Your truck on the road — commercial auto liability and physical damage.
  • Other drivers — liability when you’re at fault in a crash.
  • The customer’s vehicle on your hook — on-hook coverage while it’s being towed.
  • Vehicles sitting on your lot — garagekeepers coverage while they’re in your care.

That’s why two companies with the same truck can get very different quotes. One is buying all four pieces; the other is buying two and hoping nothing happens.

What drives your number

  • State and garaging ZIP. Where the truck sleeps matters. Theft, vandalism, accident frequency, traffic density, and litigation all vary by location — and so does the rate.
  • Truck type, age, and value. Light-duty vs. heavy-duty vs. rotator. A rotator costs more to insure than a rollback, mostly because the truck itself is worth more and the work is higher-severity. Older trucks can be cheaper on physical damage but more prone to breakdowns.
  • Driving records. MVRs are a standard pricing factor. Clean records cost less. A DUI or a string of at-fault accidents moves the number fast.
  • Insurance score. Some — not all — insurance companies base a big part of your rate on your insurance score, which is built from your credit history. It’s not the same number as your personal credit score, but they move together: late payments and high balances push it down, and that pushes your premium up. A few states restrict or ban insurance companies from using credit in rating, so whether it hits you depends on your state and your insurance company. If your credit is rough, insurance companies that don’t use insurance scores are worth quoting.
  • Radius and call volume. Local-only is cheaper than regional or multi-state. More calls, more miles, more exposure.
  • What kind of towing. Consensual roadside is one risk. Repo, impound, and rotation are different risks — involuntary tows, confrontations, stored vehicles.
  • Coverages and limits you pick. Higher liability limits, higher on-hook limits, and garagekeepers all add premium. So do low deductibles.
  • Claims history. Prior losses follow you, typically for three to five years. Small claims barely above your deductible can cost more at renewal than they pay out.
  • Time in business. New ventures usually pay more until they build a track record and show clean loss performance.
  • Lot security. Fencing, lighting, and cameras matter for garagekeepers pricing. A packed, unlit yard with prior theft losses is a surcharge waiting to happen.
  • Deductible. A higher deductible lowers premium — but only if you can actually afford to pay it out of pocket when a claim hits.

The coverage stack, in plain language

  • Commercial auto liability — pays when you’re at fault in a crash. The federal floor for for-hire interstate carriers with vehicles over 10,001 lbs GVWR is $750,000 (49 CFR 387.9). Intrastate minimums are set by your state and are often lower — but they’re the floor, not a recommendation. Most operators carry more, and rotation agreements commonly require around $1,000,000.
  • Physical damage — comp and collision on your own truck. Priced off the truck’s value. Rates tend to run 2%–8% of the total insurable value — the combined value of all your trucks. A $500,000 fleet at 4% is roughly $20,000 a year in physical damage premium alone, before any other coverage.
  • On-hook — covers the customer’s vehicle while it’s being towed. If you drop it or it’s damaged in transit, this is the coverage that pays.
  • Garagekeepers — covers vehicles in your care at your lot: theft, vandalism, fire, weather. On-hook does not cover stored vehicles.
  • Cargo — covers freight or property being transported, where it applies to your operation.
  • General liability — covers non-auto incidents at your premises and in your operations.
  • Workers’ comp — covers employee injuries. Required in most states once you have employees; Texas is the notable exception where it’s optional for most private employers.

Why quotes vary so much

Same truck, three quotes, three different numbers. Usually one of these is going on:

  • Different limits. One quote is $1M liability, another is $750K. They’re not the same policy.
  • Different coverage stacks. The cheap quote often quietly drops on-hook or garagekeepers. That’s not a better price — it’s less coverage.
  • Different insurance company appetite. Some insurance companies want towing business; some don’t. The ones that don’t price it high to make you go away.
  • Different information. A quote built on a clean driver list and two years of loss runs is a different animal than a quote built on guesses.

The only fair comparison: identical limits, identical coverages, identical driver and truck lists. Anything else is comparing a rollback to a rotator.

How you actually pay

Commercial tow policies are written as an annual premium. Most operators pay it as a down payment — a percentage of the annual premium, due up front — followed by monthly installments for the rest. The exact down payment percentage varies by insurance company and by your credit; ask for it in writing before you bind.

Paying in full usually costs less. Installment plans carry finance charges and fees that add up over the year. Some insurance companies don’t offer payment plans at all. If cash flow is tight, the monthly plan is still usually better than cutting coverage to afford the premium.

Honest ways to lower the cost

  • Keep driver records clean. The single biggest controllable factor. Hire carefully, check MVRs, and don’t put a bad driver in your best truck.
  • Raise the deductible to what you can actually afford. A higher deductible cuts premium. Just make sure you can write the check when a claim hits.
  • Secure the lot. Fencing, lighting, cameras. It helps on garagekeepers and it’s the right thing to do for your customers’ cars.
  • Dash cams and telematics. Video ends arguments. Some insurance companies offer discounts for documented safety tech, and footage has saved operators from claims that weren’t theirs.
  • Bundle the stack with one insurance company. Liability, physical damage, on-hook, and garagekeepers from one insurance company usually prices better than splitting them across three.
  • Shop with identical limits. Get every quote on the same limits and the same coverages, then pick. Don’t let a cheap quote win by quietly dropping a coverage.
  • Don’t cut on-hook or garagekeepers to save money. That’s the coverage that pays when a customer’s car is damaged on your hook or broken into on your lot. One uncovered claim wipes out years of premium savings.
  • Build a clean history. Time in business plus clean loss runs is the slowest but most reliable discount there is.

Common questions

How much does tow truck insurance cost per month?

Published 2026 rate guides put tow truck insurance at roughly $450 to $2,000 or more per truck per month, or about $5,400 to $25,000+ per year. Light-duty local work sits at the low end; heavy recovery, repo, and impound work sits at the high end. These are planning figures from published guides, not quotes — your premium comes from your operation.

Why is tow truck insurance so expensive?

Because one policy has to cover four exposures: your truck on the road, other drivers, the customer's vehicle on your hook, and vehicles sitting on your lot. Each risk has its own coverage and its own price, which is why towing costs more than a comparable delivery truck.

What is the cheapest tow truck insurance?

The cheapest quote is usually the one that leaves something out — most often on-hook or garagekeepers coverage. A policy that skips the coverage for the customer's vehicle can look cheap until the first claim. Compare identical limits and identical coverages, or the comparison is meaningless.

Can I pay tow truck insurance monthly?

Usually, yes. Most commercial policies are written as an annual premium paid as a down payment (a percentage of the annual premium) plus monthly installments. Paying in full usually costs less because installment fees add up. Some insurance companies do not offer payment plans.

What does tow truck insurance cover?

A full tow truck policy covers four things: your truck on the road (commercial auto liability and physical damage), other drivers (liability), the customer's vehicle on your hook (on-hook coverage), and vehicles sitting on your lot (garagekeepers coverage). Most operators also carry general liability and, with employees, workers' comp. What you actually need depends on the work you do — a roadside-only operator and a rotation/impound operator don't buy the same stack.
