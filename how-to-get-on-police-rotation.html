How Do I Get on a Police Rotation List? What It Pays & Requires

Rotation is a local contract, not state law. The agency’s agreement controls — so the real answer is: get that agreement, read it, and meet it. Here’s how the process works.

What rotation is

When police need a vehicle towed — a crash, an arrest, an abandoned car — and nobody requested a specific tow company, dispatch calls the next company on the agency’s rotation list. The list is run by the local law enforcement agency: city police, county sheriff, or state police / highway patrol for highways.

Rotation is steady work. It’s also the most paperwork-heavy work in towing: the agency tells you what insurance to carry, how fast to respond, what equipment to run, and what it pays. You agree in writing, and you live by that agreement.

The honest truth first

  • Every locality differs. There is no state or national rotation list, and no standard set of requirements. What one city demands, the next town over may not — and vice versa.
  • Some lists are full or closed. Agencies limit how many companies are on the list. If yours is full, ask when it opens and what the waitlist looks like.
  • Some require time in business. New Orleans, for example, requires 12 months in business in the zone before you can apply. Don’t assume a brand-new company can get on every list.
  • The agreement controls. This page gives you the typical terms from real rotation agreements. Your agency’s agreement is the one that matters — confirm its exact requirements before you apply.

Step 1: Find the right agency

Figure out which agency polices the area you want to work. City streets: city police. County roads and unincorporated areas: county sheriff. Highways: state police or highway patrol. If you want to work more than one area, you may need to apply to more than one agency — each runs its own list.

Call the agency’s non-emergency line and ask for the rotation coordinator, wrecker inspector, or whoever runs the tow program. That person is your point of contact from here on.

Step 2: Get the agreement and the application

Ask for two things: the current rotation agreement and the application. Read the agreement before you spend a dollar on anything else. It will spell out the exact insurance limits, response time, equipment standards, fees, inspection terms, and tow rates. Everything below is typical — the agreement is exact.

Step 3: Meet the common requirements

Insurance. Rotation agreements commonly require:

  • Commercial auto liability around $1,000,000 combined single limit (agency requirements range $750,000–$3,000,000)
  • On-hook / cargo $50,000–$100,000
  • Garagekeepers $50,000–$500,000
  • General liability $1,000,000–$2,000,000
  • Workers’ comp — statutory coverage for your employees
  • Agency named as additional insured, with a certificate of insurance and advance cancellation notice (commonly 30 days)

Business & legal. Business registered, tow business license / permit for your city or county, DOT number / operating authority if your operation requires it, and a driver roster — licensed drivers with clean records. Some agencies require background checks.

Equipment & operations. Trucks that meet the agency’s equipment standards (class, winch, lights, safety gear), a secure impound lot or storage yard, and 24/7 availability.

Step 4: Response time — the one that gets people kicked off

Every agreement sets a maximum response time from the call to arrival on scene. Miss it and you can be dropped to the bottom of the list, marked as a refusal, or removed. Published examples:

AgencyRequired response
Frederick, MD Police40 minutes
Morristown, NJ Police20 minutes (7am–6pm weekdays), 25 minutes at other times
New Orleans, LAMaximum 45 minutes
Denver City, TX20 minutes daytime, 30 minutes nighttime

Be honest with yourself about whether your coverage area and truck count let you meet the window. Taking a rotation slot you can’t serve is worse than waiting — it burns the relationship with the agency.

Step 5: Inspection, approval & renewal

The agency will typically inspect your trucks and lot, verify your insurance certificate, and check your paperwork. If everything passes, you sign the agreement and get added to the list. Keep your certificate of insurance current — agencies commonly require it well before renewal (Knoxville, for example, wants it at least 10 days before expiration). Many agreements renew annually; Frederick, MD requires a new application each year by December 31, and missing the deadline ends your spot on the list at midnight that day.

What rotation pays

Tow rates on rotation are usually set by the agency or by local ordinance — you don’t set them. The agreement or rate schedule will spell out what you can charge for a tow, mileage, winch-outs, storage per day, and after-hours work. Some programs charge application or participation fees. Ask for the rate schedule up front so you know whether the work pays for your operation.
