What Do I Need to Start a Tow Company?

One truck, one driver, and the right paperwork is a real starting point. Here’s the full list — and the honest warning that requirements vary by state and locality, so verify yours before you haul.

The short answer

  • Business: entity, EIN, business bank account.
  • Federal numbers: USDOT number; MC authority if you haul for hire across state lines.
  • State & local licenses: your state’s tow company license and tow operator license, plus city/county business license and tow permit.
  • Insurance: your state’s minimums at least — usually more.
  • Truck & equipment: a truck that fits the work you plan to do, with the right gear.
  • Operations: dispatch, a secure lot if you store vehicles, and a plan for where the calls come from.

1. Form the business

Pick an entity — most new tow companies form an LLC — and register it with your state. Get an EIN (Employer Identification Number) from the IRS; you’ll need it for taxes, bank accounts, and federal filings. Open a dedicated business bank account and keep business money separate from personal money from day one. Get a business phone number and a professional email — dispatch and police agencies will both need to reach you around the clock.

2. Federal numbers — if you cross state lines

If you tow across state lines, you’re in FMCSA territory:

  • USDOT number. Required for interstate commercial motor carrier operations. Many states also require a USDOT number for intrastate-only towing, so check your state even if you never leave it.
  • MC operating authority. Required if you haul for hire across state lines. New applicants apply through FMCSA’s registration system (Motus). FMCSA lists a $300 filing fee for each type of authority, and the fee is not refunded for a mistaken application — confirm you’re applying for the right authority before you pay.
  • BOC-3 process agent. You must designate a process agent in every state where you operate, or a single agent covering the whole country.
  • UCR (Unified Carrier Registration). An annual registration and fee for interstate carriers.
  • New Entrant Safety Assurance Program. New carriers go through an 18-month monitoring period with a safety audit.

Authority doesn’t go active until your insurance filing is on file with FMCSA. Don’t haul interstate loads until your status shows active.

3. State & local tow licenses

This is where it gets local. Many states and cities license tow companies and tow operators separately from the federal numbers above. The exact names, fees, and requirements vary — there is no single national tow license.

One example: Texas regulates towing through the Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR), with separate tow company and tow operator licensing. Your state may use its DOT, its public utilities commission, or a separate licensing board. Your city or county may add its own tow business license or permit on top.

Look up your state’s requirements: tow truck insurance by state →  Then call your city or county clerk and ask what local tow permits apply. That phone call saves more headaches than any website.

4. Insurance — before you haul

Insurance is usually one of the biggest first-year costs, and you need it in place before your authority goes active and before any agency or motor club will talk to you. At minimum, your state’s required coverages — and in practice, more:

  • Commercial auto liability — your state’s floor at least. The federal floor for for-hire interstate carriers with vehicles over 10,001 lbs GVWR is $750,000 (49 CFR 387.9).
  • Physical damage on your truck.
  • On-hook — the customer’s vehicle while it’s being towed.
  • Garagekeepers — vehicles stored on your lot.
  • General liability and workers’ comp if you have employees.

Your insurance company files proof of insurance with FMCSA for interstate authority, and your state may require its own filing. Check the numbers: state requirements lookup →  Figure out the stack: coverage quiz →  See what it costs: tow truck insurance cost guide →

5. Truck & equipment

Most new companies start with one truck — usually a rollback (flatbed), because it handles the widest range of everyday tows. A wrecker (wheel-lift) is the other common starter. Heavy-duty and rotator work comes later, with bigger trucks and bigger insurance bills.

  • Winch, lights, and safety gear — working winch, emergency lighting, reflective triangles or flares, chains and straps, and wheel-lift or bed equipment in good condition. Rotation programs and inspections will check this.
  • CDL. Under the federal CDL rule, a commercial driver’s license is generally required for a single vehicle with a GVWR of 26,001 pounds or more, or a combination rated over 26,000 pounds with a towed unit over 10,000 pounds. Lighter rollback and wrecker work often does not require a CDL — but your state may add its own rules, so confirm.
  • Drug & alcohol testing. CDL drivers are subject to federal drug and alcohol testing rules (49 CFR Part 382), including pre-employment testing and enrollment in the FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse. Most small carriers handle this through a testing consortium.
  • Markings. Your USDOT number and company name on the truck, where required.

6. Dispatch, lot & records

  • Dispatch. Someone has to answer the phone 24/7 if you want rotation or motor club work. A missed call is a missed tow — and on rotation, a missed call can get you dropped.
  • Secure lot. If you store vehicles — impound, police tows, accident holds — you need a secure yard: fenced, lit, ideally with cameras. It’s a garagekeepers requirement and a practical one.
  • Records. Driver qualification files, vehicle maintenance records, and daily inspection reports. FMCSA and your state can ask for them. Build the habit on day one; rebuilding records after the fact is miserable.
  • Rate sheet. Know your rates for a standard tow, a winch-out, mileage, storage per day, and after-hours. Write them down before the first call.

7. Where the calls come from

A truck with no calls is an expensive lawn ornament. The main revenue channels:

  • Police rotation. The local agency calls the next company on its list. Steady work, but every agency sets its own requirements and some lists are full. How to get on a police rotation list →
  • Motor club contracts. AAA and similar clubs dispatch roadside work to contracted towers. They set their own rates, response times, and equipment standards.
  • Private property and consent towing. Parking lots, apartment complexes, businesses — contracted or on-call.
  • Roadside and insurance dispatch. Insurance companies and roadside programs dispatch breakdowns and accidents.
  • Commercial accounts. Fleets, dealerships, repair shops, body shops. The slowest channel to build and the most stable once you have it.

Honest note: rotation and motor clubs have their own requirements and don’t accept everyone immediately. Some rotation lists require time in business. Build the paperwork first, then apply — and have a plan for private and commercial work in the meantime.

What it costs to start

There’s no single honest number. The big items are the truck, the first year of insurance, licenses and filings, and the lot. Insurance is usually one of the largest first-year costs — see the cost guide for what published ranges actually say. New ventures typically pay more for insurance until they build a track record. Budget for the down payment on the policy plus the first months of installments, not just the monthly figure.
